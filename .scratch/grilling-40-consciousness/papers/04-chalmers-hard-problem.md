# The Hard Problem: Why Consciousness Might Resist Mathematical Definition

> Chalmers, "Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness" — J. Consciousness Studies 2(3), 1995

## Core Claim

There is a **hard problem of consciousness** that is fundamentally different from the easy problems. The easy problems concern cognitive **functions** (discrimination, integration, report, attention, wakefulness) and ARE amenable to computational/neural explanation. The hard problem is the problem of **experience** — why there is "something it is like" to be a conscious organism. Chalmers argues that reductive explanation (including mathematical modeling) necessarily fails to explain experience, and that experience must be treated as **fundamental** — an irreducible posit of a complete theory of the world.

## I. The Easy/Hard Distinction

### Easy Problems (Functionally Definable)
- Discrimination and categorization of environmental stimuli
- Integration of information by a cognitive system
- Reportability of mental states
- Introspective access to internal states
- Focus of attention
- Deliberate control of behavior
- Difference between wakefulness and sleep

**Why "easy"**: For each, one specifies a neural/computational mechanism that performs the function. The function IS the thing to be explained — explaining the mechanism IS explaining the phenomenon.

### The Hard Problem (Not Functionally Definable)
**Experience itself**: the felt quality of redness, the sound of a clarinet, the pain of an injury, the warmth of an emotion. Why should physical processing give rise to a rich inner life at all?

**Why "hard"**: Even a COMPLETE functional account of the brain leaves unanswered: "Why is the performance of these functions accompanied by experience?" No amount of mechanism-description addresses this question — not because our theories are incomplete, but because the question is of a fundamentally different kind.

## II. Why Reductive Explanation Fails

### 2.1 The Entailment Criterion
Reductive explanation works when physical facts ENTAIL the facts to be explained:
- Physical facts about genes → entail facts about heredity
- Physical facts about H₂O → entail facts about liquidity

Physical facts can entail structural and functional facts. But:

> **"For any physical process we specify, it remains conceptually coherent that it be instantiated without experience."**

No entailment from physical to phenomenal is possible.

### 2.2 Current Theories Fail the Test
- **Crick & Koch's 35-75 Hz oscillations**: explain binding and working memory (functions) — nothing explains WHY oscillations produce experience
- **Baars' Global Workspace**: explains cognitive accessibility and report (functions) — nothing explains WHY global broadcasting produces experience
- **Penrose's quantum gravity**: quantum coherence in microtubules explains only further FUNCTIONS, not why those functions feel like anything
- **"Future neurophysiology"**: whatever mechanism is discovered, the same explanatory gap recurs — "From dynamics, one only gets more dynamics"

### 2.3 The Disanalogy with Vitalism
The élan vital was posited to explain life's FUNCTIONS, and was discarded when mechanisms were found. But:
> **"Experience is not an explanatory posit but an explanandum in its own right"** — it cannot be eliminated the same way, because it's the very phenomenon we're trying to explain.

## III. The Nonreductive Program

### 3.1 Naturalistic Dualism
Experience is taken as **fundamental** — an irreducible feature of the world alongside mass, charge, and spacetime. The task becomes:
1. Discover the **psychophysical principles** connecting physical processes to experience
2. Systematize these principles with mathematical elegance
3. Treat consciousness as physics treated electromagnetism: fundamental but lawful

### 3.2 The Principle of Structural Coherence
```
Consciousness structure ≅ Awareness structure (isomorphism)
```
- Awareness = "direct availability for global control" (functional correlate)
- Every structural feature of experience (similarity relations, visual field geometry, relative intensity) is mirrored in the information-processing structure
- Example: phenomenal color space is 3D (hue, saturation, intensity), and visual processing discriminates along 3 corresponding axes
- **This principle enables INDIRECT explanation of experience structure**, but not of intrinsic quality (the "redness of red")

### 3.3 The Principle of Organizational Invariance
```
∀ systems S₁, S₂: same fine-grained functional organization → same experiences
```
Proof by reductio ad absurdum ("dancing qualia"):
1. Assume two functionally identical systems differ experientially
2. Gradually replace neurons with silicon chips, one by one
3. At some tiny step, experience changes while functional organization is held constant
4. Install a toggle switch between neural and silicon circuits — flipping it changes experience
5. But the system's causal organization is unchanged, so it can NEVER NOTICE the change
6. These "dancing qualia" are absurd → the assumption must be false

### 3.4 The Double-Aspect Theory of Information
```
Information space (Shannon/Bateson: "a difference that makes a difference")
    ↙              ↘
Physical aspect    Phenomenal aspect
(embodied in       (experienced as
causal pathways)   conscious quality)
```

Both aspects are grounded in the **same abstract information space**. Experience arises as the phenomenal aspect of physically embodied information. This:
- Explains structural coherence (same information space → same structure)
- Coheres with "it from bit" (Wheeler)
- Implies a potentially panpsychist conclusion: if ALL information has a phenomenal aspect, "a thermostat... might have maximally simple experience"

## IV. Mathematical Status (for our question)

### What Chalmers' Framework ALLOWS:
- **Formal characterization of information spaces**: abstract sets of states with a difference structure
- **Precise isomorphism conditions**: between phenomenal and functional structures
- **Psychophysical laws**: mathematical relations connecting physical and phenomenal domains
- **Organizational invariance**: a well-defined equivalence class on physical systems

### What Chalmers' Framework DENIES (as possible in principle):
- **Derivation of experience from dynamics**: no set of dynamical equations can entail experiential facts
- **Mathematical definition of "what red looks like"**: structural isomorphisms capture the RELATIONS between experiences (red is more similar to orange than to blue), but not the INTRINSIC quality — "why red looks like THIS and not like the sound of a trumpet"
- **Reductive solution to the hard problem**: by the very nature of experience, no purely structural/functional account can capture it

### The Core Challenge to Grilling #40:
Chalmers' argument suggests that ANY attempt to mathematically define subjective consciousness faces a principled limit: **mathematics captures structure, and experience has an intrinsic (non-structural) dimension that structure cannot exhaust.** If this is correct, then the answer to "can subjective consciousness be mathematically defined?" is:
- **YES** — if you mean: define the structural correlates, the information space, the functional invariants
- **NO** — if you mean: derive the felt quality itself from mathematical objects alone
